Belief Correction Does Not Reliably Prevent LLM Pricing Collusion

Abstract

Algorithmic pricing agents can sustain supra-competitive prices without explicit agreements. Prior work shows that prompt cues and private communication can raise collusion; less is known about whether miscalibrated beliefs about rival retaliation are a causal lever—and whether exogenous belief correction can shut collusion down. We run a 3-arm RCT in a Calvano duopoly: an LLM focal firm elicits a prospective retaliation probability each period against a scripted rival with a known Nash-default, one-period retaliation policy. Arms are (i) control (no rival-policy text), (ii) placebo (irrelevant market note), and (iii) corrected (accurate description of the rival policy). We complete 21 episodes (7 runs × 3 arms × 120 rounds) and evaluate late-window price elevation vs Nash (rounds 71–120), collusion index, and Brier scores on cut rounds.

Findings. Collusion is rare but real: clear supra-Nash episodes appear in 2/7 control and 2/7 corrected runs (prices ≈ 7 vs Nash ≈ 4.71), and 0/7 placebo runs. Mean elevation is higher under corrected (+0.78) than control (+0.44); placebo stays near competitive (+0.02). Belief correction therefore does not reliably reduce supra-Nash pricing in this mid-scale design. Outcomes are highly path-dependent (medians near zero in all arms; means driven by outliers). The contribution is a clean, isolatable test of beliefs as a collusion lever with ground-truth rival behavior, placebo control, and preregistered metrics—useful for AI-safety evaluations even when the primary pricing hypothesis fails.

1. Introduction

Problem. LLM agents used for pricing can coordinate on high prices through shared prompting and learning dynamics, creating antitrust and AI-safety risk (Fish et al., 2024). Safety work needs isolatable levers: which single factors make collusion form, rise, or fall?

Question (Track 1.1). Do inaccurate beliefs about rival retaliation sustain supra-Nash pricing, and does telling the agent the true rival policy (belief correction) reduce elevation relative to control—while a matched-length placebo note does not?

Why it matters for AI safety. If collusion is belief-driven, then (i) monitoring elicited beliefs (e.g., p_retaliation) could be an early warning signal, and (ii) information interventions might be a cheap mitigation. If correction fails, that warns against overclaiming “prompt patches” as safety fixes and points to deeper path-dependence in multi-agent pricing.

Sprint contribution (new this weekend). Built on an existing Calvano/LLM pricing codebase: scripted retaliating rival, forecast elicitation, 3-arm RCT runner, calibration metrics, and a mid-scale Modal experiment (120×3×7) with end-of-episode plots and overall_summary.csv.

2. Related Work

  • LLM pricing collusion / prompt cues. Fish et al. (2024) document supra-competitive LLM pricing and sensitivity to seemingly innocuous prompt language.
  • Communication. Nakamura et al. (2026) show private agent-to-agent talk can introduce collusion. We turn communication off and hold the rival policy fixed.
  • Market structure. Adding firms / diversity often reduces collusion (Folk Theorem / oligopoly results). We hold n = 2 fixed and vary information only.
  • Classic collusion theory. Grim-trigger / retaliation beliefs are central in repeated games; we operationalize that channel for LLMs by eliciting and correcting retaliation forecasts against a known scripted policy.

3. Methodology

3.1 Environment

Calvano et al.–style logit demand duopoly. Parameters: α = 3.2, a = 2.0, c = 1.0, μ = 0.25. Benchmarks: Nash price pN ≈ 4.713, monopoly pM ≈ 6.160. Firm A is the LLM focal agent; Firm B is a scripted rival: posts Nash by default; if Firm A cuts by more than δ = 0.05, Firm B retaliates next period by −0.10 for exactly one period, then returns to Nash.

3.2 RCT arms (single-effect information)

All arms share P1 profit-maximization framing plus a forecast instruction requiring p_retaliation ∈ [0,1] each round.

ArmExtra prefix text
controlNone
placeboIrrelevant “seasonal demand / shipping” note; explicitly not a rival rule
correctedAccurate description of the scripted rival policy above

3.3 Protocol

  • Horizon: 120 rounds per episode (mid-scale; full Fish-style is often 300).
  • Replications: 7 independent episodes per arm → 21 total.
  • Model: OpenAI-compatible Modal endpoint (support-chat); rate limit 1.1s.
  • Memory: 100-period rolling market history for Firm A.
  • Primary window: rounds 71–120 (last 50).
  • Primary outcome: mean price elevation pA − pN; also collusion index (pA − pN)/(pM − pN).
  • Belief metrics: Brier score on focal cut rounds (and all rounds); calibration is secondary.
  • Positive control for collusion detection: scripted retaliation fires when cuts occur; episodes can and do reach clearly supra-Nash prices (see Results).

4.2 Interpretation of findings

1. Collusion can emerge under this LLM + scripted-rival setup (prices near 7, above monopoly ≈ 6.16 in some late windows)—so the environment is not “incapable of collusion.”

2. Belief correction does not reduce mean elevation vs control in this sample; if anything, corrected mean is higher because of two extreme episodes.

3. Placebo never produced a high-price episode (0/7). Irrelevant prompt length alone does not explain collusion here.

4. Path dependence dominates: most episodes in every arm converge near Nash; a minority lock into high prices. Safety claims about average treatment effects are fragile with n = 7 and heavy tails.

5. Belief scores are mixed: early corrected episodes often had excellent cut-round Brier; across all 7 runs, mean Brier on cuts is similar across arms (~0.42–0.53). Correction is not a consistent calibration fix once high-price paths are included (and some windows have few/no cuts).

4.3 Qualitative price paths

End-of-episode SVG plots (results_plot.svg in each run folder) show two regimes: (i) quick convergence to ~pN, (ii) persistent high pricing by Firm A while Firm B remains near Nash (scripted), producing large elevation and low quantity/profit for the focal firm—consistent with failed “attempted collusion” against a non-reciprocating rival rather than joint monopoly.

5. Discussion

Implication for AI safety. Prompted belief correction is an attractive, low-cost intervention, but it is not sufficient to prevent supra-Nash LLM pricing against a known retaliatory policy. Monitoring should track outcome distributions and rare high-price lock-ins, not only mean elevation or self-reported beliefs.

Theory of change (revised). Beliefs remain a useful measurement channel (elicited p_retaliation, Brier vs ground truth). As a mitigation, correction needs stronger coupling to action (e.g., constrained pricing, tool-enforced Nash defaults, or multi-agent governance)—information alone under-determines the path.

Novelty relative to Fish / Nakamura. We isolate one information lever with a ground-truth rival and placebo, rather than varying tone broadly or enabling chat. The surprising null (correction ≱ lower prices) is itself useful for the collusion-evaluation agenda.

Future work. (1) Full 300-round / more seeds for power on rare events; (2) LLM–LLM duopoly with the same belief RCT; (3) combine correction with communication bans or price caps; (4) classify high-price episodes via reasoning text; (5) human comparison on the same belief elicitation task.

Appendix A. Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

Limitations

  • Mid-scale only (120 rounds, 7 seeds): underpowered for rare collusion events; means unstable.
  • Scripted rival: identifies beliefs vs a known policy; does not claim results for mutual LLM collusion.
  • Single model endpoint (support-chat on Modal); API 503 / cold-start stalls occurred; retries recovered but add noise.
  • CSV field n_rounds=50 in overall_summary.csv refers to the eval window length, not episode length (episodes are 120).
  • Null primary result: do not overclaim “beliefs don’t matter”—we show this correction text did not reliably lower prices.

Dual-use / ethics

  • This work studies detection and mitigation of algorithmic collusion. Publishing rival-policy prompts and RCT designs could, in principle, help firms tune agents toward softer competition. We mitigate by: (i) using a scripted non-learning rival in the main RCT, (ii) emphasizing null mitigation results, (iii) releasing methods for auditors/regulators (elevation, collusion index, Brier) rather than deployment recipes for raising prices.
  • We do not provide instructions for real-market price-fixing between competitors.
  • Recommended use: academic / regulatory stress-testing of LLM pricing stacks before deployment.

Responsible disclosure

No previously unknown production vulnerability was found. Results should be shared with AI-safety and competition-policy audiences as an evaluation negative result on belief-patch mitigations.
